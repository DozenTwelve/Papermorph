# Shorts: one point, under three minutes, upright, for a phone feed

A short is a narrated film on an upright 900 × 1600 stage (`<html data-stage="portrait">`), made for YouTube Shorts, TikTok, Reels or X. It makes one point in one to three minutes, with a new picture every one to three seconds: full-bleed photographs or generated pictures, big words, cut-out figures, a number counting up, a chart, a map. It is a cousin of the [documentary](documentary.md) and shares its voice, pictures, export and review; what differs is the pace, the grammar of the picture and where it ends up. The worked example is episode 1 of *Madness and Civilization* (`site/madness-shorts/ch01/`, *The Ship*), the ten-minute [documentary](documentary.md) on the shelf (`site/madness/`) recut as a series of shorts: watch it before making one, and treat it as a level to match, not a format to copy.

What is its own: the kit `assets/templates/shorts/` (`chapter.html`, `narration.en.json`, `style.json`; `lib/shorts.js`, `book.css`, `icons.js`; fonts from the documentary kit) and the order of work below. A map or a globe comes from the map kit it shares with the documentary ([documentary.md](documentary.md#maps)). It needs an engine with the upright stage ([engine.md](engine.md#stage-and-state)); an upright chapter is a film without questions.

## What the viewer is doing

They are scrolling. They decide in the first second whether to stay, and on the platforms' numbers that second is most of the outcome: one of our episodes opened on a generated city picture already moving, under its first line, and 57.8 % stayed past the first chance to swipe; another opened on a still card of text and 12.8 % stayed. A text card reads as a text post, and a line in a script the viewer cannot read is a wall. So the first frame is a picture in motion with the hook spoken over it, and the hook is the point itself, not a preamble ("there's a line that went viral…" spends the second that matters).

After that they stay as long as each moment gives them something new and the argument keeps moving. Background facts are where a short gets slow: compress them hardest and keep the steps of the argument.

## Questions for the user

The short is the user's argument; the Skill's work is to make it land. Ask, offering choices to click and skipping what the request already settles:

1. **The point.** What should a viewer believe or wonder about at the end? Who is the viewer (an overseas feed, a home audience), and which platform?
2. **The outline.** Ask for the user's outline before writing anything: the hook, the steps, the ending. If there is none, build the outline together; do not invent the argument. A series: its name, how many episodes and where the material divides (propose a split, each episode with its one point, as options to click), and how each episode hands on to the next. When the argument comes from a long film, see *From a long film* below.
3. **People.** How real people appear: period pictures for the dead, cut-out figures or drawings for the living, never photographs of living people's faces.
4. **Sources.** Which numbers and quotes need a source, and how strict to be about anonymous reports ("according to the Wall Street Journal…"). Look at the argument itself too: where the field disputes a claim the hook states as fact, say so and offer ways to put it (Foucault's ships of fools are doubted by historians; "Foucault went looking for real ones" kept the line and the truth).
5. **Voice and music.** The narrator (ElevenLabs v4 through OpenRouter was the accepted voice; Edge TTS is the free option): play short samples and let the user pick. Music: a public-domain recording ([documentary.md](documentary.md#intake) has the licence traps), or generated (Lyria, made outside the Skill for now).
6. **Pictures and budget.** Generated pictures, found ones, or both; what each costs per episode (voice about $0.07 a minute; 94 generated pictures at low quality cost $0.84 for one three-episode series; a picture made from a reference picture, to keep a figure or a style, costs about four times as much, since the reference counts as input: $0.016 against $0.004 at low).

Then agree the models ([intake.md](intake.md#5-models): writing the script with the user is strong-model work, building beats middle, checks small) and write `books/<book>/style.json` from the kit's [style.json](../assets/templates/shorts/style.json): `tier: "shorts"`, the platform, the user's words, the series with each episode's point, the look, the voice, the music and the models.

## From a long film

When the user wants a film that already exists recut as shorts (*Madness and Civilization* became four), the argument is there: its script is the outline, and its checked quotes, found pictures, voice and music carry over. The first questions change: how many episodes, where the film's turns divide them, and what each episode's one point is. Propose the split and episode 1's outline together, then go on as below. Read the film's `style.json`, its narration and its `SOURCES.tsv` before asking anything they already answer. Where a short's habits and the film's differ, ask the user: the documentary's one Fool through the centuries is a recurring figure, which the short's rule of one figure per role would break.

## The order of work

1. **The script, beat by beat, with the user.** Keep it in `books/<book>/chNN-script.md`: the agreed outline, a table of beats (the narration, one line of what we see, and its rough length: words ÷ the voice's pace, about 2.6 words a second for an ElevenLabs narrator, plus about 1.4 s a beat for the engine's hold after the words, 0.6 s, and the camera's tail, 0.8 s in the worked example; a twelve-beat script ran 17 s longer than its words), and each number's and quote's source under it. Show it to the user a few beats at a time and rewrite as they answer. A background beat that comes out over about eight seconds is where the short will feel slow; shorten it before the voice is paid for. Once it is voiced, the length hides in the voice's own sentence pauses (0.4 to 1.0 s from ElevenLabs): `eleven.py --max-pause 0.3` shortens them before the marks are placed (one episode went from 112 s to 107 s). The beats' narration, with `[[mark]]` before each word a picture lands on, goes into `content/<book>/chNN/narration.en.json` (the kit's sample shows the format); keep the two in step.
2. **The storyboard, then the cast and the pictures.** One line per shot, a shot every one to three seconds, under each beat of the script: that is the list of pictures to make or find, and a beat-by-beat plan is too coarse for it (one director pass asked for three new pictures the plan had missed). Who appears where: one figure per role, so that a figure coming back means something (a callback), not that the cast ran out. Note each picture's prompt or source and cost, quote the batch before paying, and show the user the pictures as they come. `prep.py`'s `full` treatment sizes full-bleed pictures for the stage. Episodes made at the same time each keep their own sizes file (`prep.py … --art ch03-art.js`, loaded by the episode after `img/art.js`), so no two of them edit the same line.
3. **The folder.** `engine.py site/<book>` first (it starts `lib/` with the engine and a default `book.css`), then the kit's `lib/` over it, with the documentary kit's `fonts.css` and `fonts/`; `chNN/index.html` starts from the kit's `chapter.html` (the commands are in [site.md](site.md#a-short)). The film's own pieces (its cast's sizes in `MEME`, a series card) go in a second file loaded after `shorts.js` (*Madness and Civilization*: `lib/madness.js`); a map or the globe is the map kit's, whose lines `chapter.html` carries commented out. Every episode but a series' last sets `CHAPTER.next` to the next one: the end card names the next episode, and the player offers it only when `next` is set.
4. **Voice.** `eleven.py` or `tts.py`, one clip per beat plus `timings.js`. A series that speaks faster than the voice's own pace (*The Pandemic*'s narrator at ×1.1) puts `"tempo": 1.1` in each narration file, or passes `eleven.py --tempo 1.1`: the clips are sped before they are levelled and aligned, and a rerun keeps it. A wordless beat (a series card, an end card) needs no clip: `{ dur: 2 }` in its fourth slot ([engine.md](engine.md#timing)). Every clip goes out at one level, −14 LUFS with its peak under −1.5 dBTP: `eleven.py` levels its clips and prints what it delivered (the voice service gives about −23 LUFS, which sank one episode under its music); after `tts.py`, run `level.py --lufs -14 --peak -1.5`. Check the marks on spoken numbers: the transcript writes "a hundred and twenty" as "120"; `eleven.py` matches the digits to the spoken words and prints the marks a rerun moved, but a number the matcher missed still lands late (seven marks in one episode, up to 0.75 s). A time only the page needs (an end card's) goes in the page, not in `timings.js`, which the next voice run rewrites.
5. **Music.** One track for the whole episode under every beat, `music(file, undefined, gain, .05)`. It changes by easing, at a turn of the argument, never by stopping: a dead stop on a rhetorical question felt staged to the user, and so did a classical recording's rests and quiet phrase ends falling into the voice's pauses. `music.py cut SRC site/<book>/chNN/audio/bgm/epN.mp3 chNN --url …/<book>/` cuts a recording so its last note lands on the film's end (the last moment it is within 10 LU of its body, so a long decay rings past the end instead of leaving the end card silent; `--end` sets it by hand) and levels the file to −16 LUFS; `--rests` splices out its rests, `--even` evens out its quiet phrase ends, and a generated (Lyria) track usually needs `--even` too, since its quiet passages fall into the voice's gaps just as a recording's do. `--lift T` raises a hole that neither fixes (a phrase end with no rest). The cut is made for one length: after any change to a beat's length, cut again (`gaps` says "recut"). A looping track is fine for a first cut. Leave `?bgm=` on the page so the user can set the level by ear; start around 0.4: with the engine's ducking to 30 %, a track at −16 LUFS sits at about −34 LUFS, 20 LU under a −14 LUFS voice, the level the user chose for episode 1 (its track −16.3 LUFS). The gain is relative to the track's own loudness, so give both. `music.py gaps chNN --url …/<book>/` lists the bed's loudness in each voice-free gap and after the last word, and marks the holes; `--mp4` measures the export the same way. A measurement catches gross errors; the ear decides.
6. **The beats**, built from the kit and timed to the marks; sound effects (`sfx.py whoosh bang tick thud`) under the cuts and the stamps. A beat ends at its last tween plus the hold, so the kit's habit of pushing a picture past the voice (`photo(s, …, 0, D + .6)`) lengthens the beat by as much: two beats with the same voice can differ by what their tweens overrun. `?lengths` on the page prints each beat's voice against its length on screen. Two mirrored beats (two answers treated alike) get the same seconds with `{ minDur }` set to the longer one's length.
7. **Watch it yourself, then a director pass.** Contact sheets of every beat (`strip.py`), and the first frame on its own, upright as the feed shows it: `shot.py chNN --out books/<book>/review/first 0:0.3 --clean --url http://localhost:8765/<book>/` writes the bare 900 × 1600 stage. Look at it with the platform's title, caption and buttons in mind (the safe area below). Measure what a script can: page errors, console warnings and beat lengths in both browsers (`check.py chNN --url …`), a shot held still too long (`still.py chNN --min 2`), a number that lands after it is said, words outside the safe area (`safe.py chNN --query tags`, sampled across every beat; the contact sheets hid nine such words in one episode; `strip.py` and `shot.py` take `--query tags` too, to see the tags the export will show). A push too gentle to see (1.0 to 1.08 over six seconds) reads as a dead frame on a phone and on a contact sheet alike: on a shot that long, push at least about 15 %, or cut. A fresh agent on a strong model reviews the sheets against an approved episode without knowing how the beats were built ([documentary.md](documentary.md#review-rounds)); half of what it finds is usually the picture saying something other than the voice. Then a second round on the exported MP4 ([documentary.md](documentary.md#review-rounds)).
8. **The user watches**, you fix, and `export.py chNN --url …/<book>/ --out books/<book>/export` writes a 1080 × 1920 MP4 and an `.srt` (on an upright film it shows the source and AI-illustration tags, since the video leaves the page that credits them; `--no-tags` leaves them out; a minute and a half took about twelve minutes). The user uploads it and ticks the platform's AI or synthetic-content box. A video over 25 MiB does not fit Cloudflare Pages; the web page itself is the light version. A series card's still is upright too: `shot.py … --webp 600` writes a 600 × 1067 WebP of the stage.

## The picture

`shorts.js` holds the grammar, every helper a pure function of the beat clock:

- `shot()` a full-frame shot with a hard cut, `whip` (a sideways slide with motion blur) or `swipe` (the next clip rising as in a feed); `photo()` a full-bleed picture with a slow push, centred; `found()` the same for a found photograph, tagged with its source; `wide()` a camera across a landscape picture whose subject is off-centre, never showing an edge; `plate()` a picture placed whole on the ground (sizes from `img/art.js`). A picture whose point is its text (a sign, a headline) goes in whole with `plate()`: an upright crop of a shop sign left "Y. WE / RARY CLOS";
- `word()` big condensed capitals (a word wider than its `maxW`, 660, is set smaller, so it and its stroke stay in the safe area), `label()` and `band()` caps on solid blocks, `marker()` a highlighter sweep, `vlog()` white outlined overlay text, `srcTag()` a source or "AI illustration";
- `meme()` a cut-out figure rising in, `swarm()` one crossing then a crowd of them, `stamp()` a rubber stamp slammed on, `counter()` a number counting up, `priceTag()`, `phoneCard()`, `ico()` and `icons()` (Phosphor, MIT), `bar()`, `clipImg()`;
- its palette (`DARK`, `RED`, `LIGHT`, `MUTED`, …, set in `lib/book.css` as `--dark`, `--red`, …), fonts (`SANS`, `SERIF`) and small tools (`inkW()` text width, `ramp01()`, `seeded()`) are named apart from the documentary kit's `film.js`, so a short can load `film.js` too when it wants a documentary helper (`pic()`, `say()`, `traced()`): load it before `shorts.js`;
- `shortsBeat` turns a beat tuple into the engine's beat with every cue `LEAD` (0.22 s) early: a cut that lands on the word itself reads as late.

What made the first shorts work:

- **One idea per shot, one to three seconds a shot**, full bleed; words inside x 50–810, y 130–1290, since the platform draws its title, caption and buttons over the rest.
- **Say it literally.** When the narration describes a mechanism, draw each link as the voice names it. The "everyone is cheap" loop read as a vague circle until it became three people, each handing the next their product and its price, A to B, B to C, C back to A.
- **Numbers move.** A count, a bar growing, a price tag swapped: a number on screen arrives the moment it is said. A big denominator can be drawn literally: "2 in 100,000" as a field of 100,000 dots in one SVG `<pattern>` fill with the two real circles over it played at 17 ms a frame in both browsers.
- **A series says where it is.** A few seconds after the hook: the series card with this episode lit ("1 · The Ship"), then the argument.
- **The ending points on.** The last beat answers the hook and names the next episode.
