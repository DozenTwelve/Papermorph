Apollo 13: The Way Home

Papermorph: a scroll story

In April 1970 three astronauts were on their way to the Moon when their spaceship broke. This is how they, and a whole team on Earth, got them home.

Scroll down to start; scroll back to rewind.

↓

11 April 1970, Florida. A giant Saturn V rocket lifts off with three astronauts on top.

Their plan: to land on the Moon, the third landing ever.

Jim Lovell is the commander. Fred Haise will fly the Moon lander. Jack Swigert will fly the capsule that brings them home.

Swigert joined the crew only a few days before launch, when the astronaut he replaced, Ken Mattingly, was kept home in case he caught German measles.

Their spaceship has three parts, joined nose to nose.

Odyssey is the capsule that will come home. Behind it, the service module carries their power, their oxygen and the big engine. At the front is Aquarius, the Moon lander.

For two days, everything goes well. A long push from the rocket sends them toward the Moon.

On the second day a small push puts them on the path for the landing: a path that would not bring them back to Earth by itself. Later pushes would do that.

The drawing is not to scale: the real Moon is much farther away.

56 hours after launch, Swigert switches on fans to stir an oxygen tank, a normal job.

But inside the tank a wire had been damaged before the flight. It starts a fire, the tank bursts, and a whole side panel of the service module is blown off.

Nobody on board was hurt. The ship was.

They hear a bang. Then they call Houston:

“Okay, Houston, we’ve had a problem here.” Jack Swigert

“This is Houston. Say again, please.” Jack Lousma, Houston

“Houston, we’ve had a problem.” Jim Lovell

NASA’s recording, with the pauses shortened (9 s).

Over the next two hours, the oxygen in both tanks leaks away into space.

The service module made electricity from that oxygen. Now it can make none. Odyssey still has batteries, but they are needed for the very end: the landing.

The Moon lander becomes a lifeboat. Lovell and Haise switch Aquarius on. Swigert switches Odyssey off, to save its batteries.

Aquarius was built to keep two people alive for two days. Mission Control works out how it can keep three going for four.

First, a way back. Hour 61: Aquarius fires its engine for 34 seconds.

Now the Moon’s pull will swing them around behind it and send them back toward Earth.

Hour 77. They pass behind the Moon. There will be no landing, but they see its far side, a side no one on Earth can see.

Out here they are farther from Earth than any people had ever been. Nobody went farther until Artemis II, in 2026.

Two hours later, a second push, much longer: 4 minutes and 24 seconds.

It gets them home about 9 hours sooner, and aims them at the Pacific Ocean, where ships are waiting.

NASA’s mission report; other accounts say 10 or 12 hours, against different plans.

Then, a problem with the air. Every breath out adds a gas called carbon dioxide. Filter cartridges soak it up.

Aquarius has filters for two people. Odyssey has plenty of spares, but they are square, and Aquarius’s holder is round.

In Houston, engineers build a way to connect them, using only things the crew have on board: plastic covers torn from their instruction books, bags, hoses and tape.

They try it on the ground first.

Then Joe Kerwin in Houston reads the steps out over the radio, for about an hour, and Swigert and Haise build it in space.

They call it “the mailbox”. The carbon dioxide starts to fall.

The ship drifts a little off course. Hour 105: with the computer off to save power, they steer by hand, keeping the line between day and night on Earth steady in the window, for a 14-second push.

Hour 137: one last small push.

All the way home they save power. Nearly everything is switched off, and the cabin gets as cold as a fridge: 3 °C.

The chart shows Aquarius’s batteries. Full at hour 58; about a fifth left when they let it go.

NASA mission report: 2,179 amp-hours at the start, 410 at the end. The line between is read by eye from the report’s chart.

Hour 138. Near Earth, they let the broken service module go.

As it drifts away they see the damage for the first time, and photograph it: the whole side panel is gone.

Hour 140. They wake Odyssey up, one step at a time, from a plan written on the ground by flight controller John Aaron, Ken Mattingly and many engineers.

Then they climb in, and let Aquarius go. The lifeboat has done its job.

Odyssey drops into the air, hot as a shooting star behind its heat shield. For six minutes the radio is silent. Then: parachutes.

17 April 1970. Splashdown in the South Pacific Ocean, 6.5 km from the ship USS Iwo Jima. All three are home.

Three people were in danger. It took a whole team to bring them home: the crew, and the flight controllers and engineers on the ground.

“A fine example of cooperation between ground and space.”
Jim Lovell, about the mailbox

The President gave the Medal of Freedom to the crew, and to the Mission Operations Team.
