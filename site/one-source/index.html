One article, four shapes

A film, three shorts, a page to explore and a scroll story, all from one Wikipedia article.

The source

Everything starts from one article: “Apollo 13” on English Wikipedia, pinned to revision 1386441839 of 18 November 2026 and shared under CC BY-SA 4.0. The pictures and the radio recordings are NASA’s own, in the public domain.

Before anything was made, 25 events of the mission were each traced from the article’s sentence to NASA’s papers: the Mission Report of September 1970, the air-to-ground transcript and the Apollo Flight Journal. Where they disagree, all four shapes follow NASA.

  • Two burnsThe article gives 105:18:42 and 137:40:13, the times the two engine burns ended. NASA’s report has them starting at 105:18:28 and 137:39:51.
  • The famous callThe article puts Lovell’s “Houston, we’ve had a problem” at 55:55:42. The original transcript has it seven seconds earlier, at 55:55:35.
  • The farthest pointTwo published distances disagree, 400,171 km and 400,046 km. So the pages say “farther from Earth than anyone had ever been” and leave the number out.

Four shapes

The same facts, pictures and credits go into all four. What changes is the pace, the frame, and who is in control.

The documentary

To watch, start to finish. It opens on nine seconds of the real air-to-ground recording, then a narrator tells how a Moon landing became a rescue.

Pace
The film’s: 4.6 minutes
Frame
A wide screen, narrated, with captions
Control
Play and pause

Three upright shorts

For a phone feed. Each makes one point and stands on its own: the lander that became a lifeboat, the two engine pushes, the square filter made to fit a round hole.

Pace
1:34, 1:24 and 1:32, each under 100 seconds
Frame
Upright, narrated, big labels and close crops
Control
Play; each episode leads on to the next

The Earth-Moon path

To find out what happened where. The reader drags the mission clock, or presses Play, along a drawn path around the Moon and home, and opens each stop for its pictures and its source.

Pace
The reader’s: 8 stops
Frame
A labelled drawing, not to scale; the real recordings when the reader asks
Control
The reader drags, stops and compares three paths

The scroll story

To read at your own speed. Words on one side, and a picture that changes as you scroll: the launch, the broken ship, the fix for the air, the splashdown.

Pace
The reader’s: 20 steps
Frame
Text beside NASA photographs and four drawings
Control
Scrolling sets the pace; scroll back to rewind

One ledger of facts, four tellings. A film decides when you see each thing; a page to explore lets you decide; a scroll story sits in between.

How it was made

The research and all four shapes were made over two days, 27 and 28 November 2026. Codex, OpenAI’s coding agent, gathered and checked the research: it pinned the article, traced the 25 events to NASA’s papers, and collected the pictures and recordings. Claude, Anthropic’s model, made the four shapes from the Papermorph Skill and that checked research. Codex reviewed every change.

The documentary and the shorts were exported as video and watched by a fresh Claude, with no memory of making them, as a director; its notes were fixed. Then a person watched every shape and approved it before it went on the shelf.
